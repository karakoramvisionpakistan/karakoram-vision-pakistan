<h2><strong>What the trek actually demands</strong></h2>
<p>The <a href="/trekking/k2-base-camp-trek/">K2 base camp trek</a> is not a climb. There is no rope work, no crampons on the standard route, and no technical skill required. What it does demand is endurance: roughly two weeks of walking six to eight hours a day, much of it on the broken moraine of the Baltoro Glacier, sleeping above 4,000m for several consecutive nights, in tents, far from any road.</p>
<p>People who struggle on this trek are rarely unfit in the gym sense. They are usually people who have never walked for many days in a row, or who arrive underestimating the altitude.</p>

<h2><strong>Training: start four months out</strong></h2>
<p>The single most useful preparation is walking uphill with a loaded pack, repeatedly. Build it like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Months 1 and 2:</strong> three or four sessions a week. Two of cardio - running, cycling, swimming, 45 minutes each. One longer walk at the weekend, three to four hours, with a 6 to 8kg pack.</li>
<li><strong>Month 3:</strong> increase the weekend walk to six hours over hilly ground with 10kg. Add one session of leg and core strength - squats, lunges, step-ups, planks.</li>
<li><strong>Month 4:</strong> walk on consecutive days. Two long days back to back, every second weekend, is the closest thing to the real experience. Descents matter too, so find terrain that goes down as well as up.</li>
<li><strong>Final two weeks:</strong> reduce the load and let your body recover. Arriving tired helps nobody.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you live somewhere flat, stairs, a treadmill on incline, or a loaded pack on long walks all work. Consistency beats intensity.</p>

<h2><strong>Altitude: the part you cannot train for</strong></h2>
<p>You cannot train for altitude at sea level, and nobody knows in advance how they will respond to it. What you can do is follow a sensible profile, and our itinerary is built around exactly that: a gradual gain from Askole at around 3,000m to Concordia at about 4,600m and the K2 base camp area higher still, with rest and acclimatisation days built in.</p>
<p>Three rules matter on the trail. Walk slowly - slower than feels natural, especially on the first days. Drink far more than you think you need, three to four litres a day. Report headaches, nausea or unusual breathlessness to the guide early; mild symptoms treated early are routine, ignored ones are not.</p>
<p>If you want to discuss altitude medication, do it with your own doctor before you travel, and bring whatever you agree on in its original packaging.</p>

<h2><strong>Gear that matters most</strong></h2>
<p>The full list is on our <a href="/equipment-checklist/">equipment checklist</a>, but four items decide whether the trek is comfortable:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Boots.</strong> Broken in over at least a hundred kilometres before you fly. The moraine is sharp, uneven and relentless.</li>
<li><strong>Sleeping bag.</strong> Rated comfortably below freezing. Nights at Concordia are cold in any month.</li>
<li><strong>Trekking poles.</strong> They take load off the knees on glacier descents and help enormously on loose ground.</li>
<li><strong>Sun protection.</strong> Glacier glasses, a high-factor sunscreen and lip balm. Reflected sun on ice burns faster than almost anywhere else.</li>
</ul>
<p>Porters carry your duffle up to an agreed weight; you walk with a daypack holding water, layers, camera and anything you need during the day.</p>

<h2><strong>Insurance and evacuation</strong></h2>
<p>This is not optional and it is not the place to save money. Your policy must cover trekking to the maximum altitude on your itinerary, and it must cover helicopter evacuation. Read the altitude clause specifically - many standard policies stop at 4,000m, which is below where you will be walking for days.</p>
<p>In Pakistan, helicopter rescue is arranged through the authorities and normally requires a financial guarantee in advance. We will explain how this works for your group before departure, and we need your insurance details before the trek starts.</p>

<h2><strong>Permits and paperwork</strong></h2>
<p>The route passes through Central Karakoram National Park, so park fees and trekking registration apply, and foreign trekkers are registered before leaving Skardu. We handle the permits, the registration and the Askole road arrangements - what we need from you is your passport details and arrival dates well in advance.</p>

<h2><strong>Food, water and the small things</strong></h2>
<p>Our cook travels with the group and meals are cooked fresh at camp - the food is better than most people expect. Water is boiled or treated; carry a bottle you can refill rather than relying on plastic. Tell us about dietary requirements when you book, not at the first camp, as supplies are carried in from Skardu.</p>
<p>There is no mobile signal beyond Askole. Satellite communication is carried by the team for operational and emergency use. Tell your family before you leave that silence for two weeks is normal and expected.</p>

<h2><strong>The mental side</strong></h2>
<p>Two weeks without a shower, without a bed, without connectivity, walking in the same direction every day. Most people find this becomes the best part of the trip somewhere around day five. But it helps to expect simplicity rather than comfort, to bring a book, and to accept that weather may change the plan. Spare days exist in the itinerary for that reason.</p>

<h2><strong>Your timeline</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Six months out:</strong> choose dates, confirm the trek, start walking regularly.</li>
<li><strong>Four months out:</strong> begin the structured training above; book insurance and read the altitude clause.</li>
<li><strong>Three months out:</strong> buy boots and start breaking them in; apply for the visa with our invitation letter.</li>
<li><strong>One month out:</strong> final gear check, medical check, confirm what you will hire in Skardu.</li>
<li><strong>Two weeks out:</strong> taper your training, weigh your bags, send us any final questions.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you are unsure whether you are ready, tell us honestly about your walking history and we will tell you honestly whether this route or a shorter one such as <a href="/trekking/rakaposhi-base-camp-trek/">Rakaposhi base camp</a> is the better first step.</p>
