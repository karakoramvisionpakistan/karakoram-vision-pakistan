<h2><strong>Why this matters here</strong></h2>
<p>Gilgit-Baltistan is a small, high, fragile place. Villages sit on irrigated land built by hand over generations; water comes from glaciers that are visibly changing; waste does not decompose easily at altitude and there is almost no system for removing it. Tourism has grown quickly, and how visitors behave now will decide what the region looks like in twenty years.</p>
<p>None of what follows is difficult. Most of it costs nothing.</p>

<h2><strong>Waste: carry it out</strong></h2>
<p>There is no rubbish collection on a trekking route, and burying waste at altitude does nothing - it stays there, often for decades. On our treks and expeditions, the team carries out what it carries in, including kitchen waste and packaging.</p>
<p>What you can do: remove excess packaging before you fly, carry a small bag for your own wrappers and tissues, and resist the urge to leave anything &quot;biodegradable&quot; behind. Orange peel does not biodegrade on a glacier.</p>
<p>In villages and towns, the simplest contribution is to avoid single-use plastic. A refillable bottle removes dozens of bottles from a two-week trip.</p>

<h2><strong>Water</strong></h2>
<p>Water from the glacier looks clean and often is, but it is also the drinking and irrigation supply for the villages below. Wash at least fifty metres from any stream, use biodegradable soap sparingly or none at all, and never use detergent directly in running water.</p>
<p>On trek, the camp boils water for the group. A filter bottle or purification tablets as backup means you never need to buy bottled water on the road.</p>

<h2><strong>Toilets</strong></h2>
<p>Where there is a toilet tent, use it. Where there is not, go well away from water and paths, dig down, and carry out all paper and hygiene products in a sealed bag. This is the single most common failure on popular routes, and it is the one visitors notice most on arrival at a busy camp.</p>

<h2><strong>Porters and staff</strong></h2>
<p>Your trek depends on people carrying loads at altitude. Responsible operators have agreed load limits, provide proper clothing and footwear for high and cold sections, provide shelter and food at camp, and carry insurance for staff. We follow these standards, and you are welcome to ask us exactly how they apply on your route.</p>
<p>What travellers can do: keep your duffle within the agreed weight, learn a few names, do not expect staff to carry personal items beyond the agreement, and tip through the guide at the end so it is shared fairly rather than handed out unevenly along the trail.</p>

<h2><strong>Photography and people</strong></h2>
<p>Ask before photographing people, particularly women and children, and accept no gracefully. A smile and a gesture toward the camera is usually enough to make the question clear. If you promise to send a photograph, send it - people remember.</p>
<p>Do not give sweets, pens or money to children. It encourages begging from passing vehicles and undermines school attendance. If you want to contribute, ask your guide about the local school or health post and give there instead.</p>

<h2><strong>Dress and customs</strong></h2>
<p>Gilgit-Baltistan is relaxed and welcoming, and it is also conservative. Loose clothing covering shoulders and knees is appropriate for men and women alike in villages and towns. A scarf is useful for women at religious sites. During Ramadan, avoid eating and drinking openly in public during daylight.</p>
<p>Ask before entering mosques, shrines or private courtyards. Permission is almost always given, and asking is what matters.</p>

<h2><strong>Spending where it stays</strong></h2>
<p>The money that helps most is the money that stays in the valley: local guest houses, family-run hotels, village cooks, local drivers and porters, and craft bought directly from the people who made it. Bargaining hard over small amounts in a village shop saves you very little and costs the seller a great deal.</p>
<p>Choosing a locally based operator has the same effect. We are based in Skardu, our guides, drivers and cooks are from these valleys, and the wages stay here.</p>

<h2><strong>Wildlife</strong></h2>
<p>Deosai is home to the Himalayan brown bear, a protected and still-recovering population. If you see one, stay in the vehicle, keep your distance, and do not approach or try to attract it for a photograph. The same applies to ibex and markhor in the side valleys. Feeding wild animals, and leaving food waste that attracts them to campsites, puts both animals and people at risk.</p>

<h2><strong>Sacred and sensitive places</strong></h2>
<p>Rock carvings, old shrines and ancient Buddhist sites are scattered along the Indus and the Karakoram Highway, many unprotected. Do not climb on them, mark them, or take fragments. Some areas near borders and sensitive installations have photography restrictions - your guide will tell you where, and that advice is worth following exactly.</p>

<h2><strong>A short checklist</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li>Refillable water bottle; no single-use plastic.</li>
<li>Carry out everything, including paper and peel.</li>
<li>Wash away from streams; minimal soap.</li>
<li>Ask before photographs; no sweets or money to children.</li>
<li>Loose, modest clothing in villages.</li>
<li>Tip through the guide, fairly and at the end.</li>
<li>Keep your distance from wildlife.</li>
<li>Buy and stay local where you can.</li>
</ul>
<p>Travelling this way does not make a trip harder. It usually makes it better, because it opens doors - an invitation to tea, a conversation, a name remembered the next year. If you want to know how we work with porters and communities, our <a href="/social-responsibility/">social responsibility</a> page sets it out, and you are always welcome to ask us directly.</p>
