faqs:
  - question: How difficult is the Gondogoro La crossing?
    answer: >-
      This is the hardest of our trekking routes. The pass itself is a pre-dawn climb on snow and
      ice with fixed ropes, harness and crampons, led by climbing guides. Everything below the pass
      is normal Baltoro trekking, but the crossing day demands fitness and a head for steep ground.
  - question: Is the pass always open?
    answer: >-
      No. It opens only when the snow, ice and rope conditions allow, usually from mid-July to late
      August, and the decision is made on the mountain by the guides. If the pass is closed the
      group returns down the Baltoro the way it came, which is why the itinerary allows spare days.
  - question: Do I need climbing experience?
    answer: >-
      Previous glacier or via ferrata experience helps, but is not essential. Our guides fix the
      ropes and brief the team beforehand. What matters more is strong trekking fitness and comfort
      on exposed, steep snow.
  - question: How is this different from the K2 base camp trek?
    answer: >-
      The first part is the same — Askole, Baltoro, Concordia and K2 base camp. Instead of walking
      back the same way, this route crosses Gondogoro La into the Hushe valley, giving the famous
      view of K2, Broad Peak and the Gasherbrums from the pass.

  - day: '01'
    title: Arrive in Islamabad
    description: |-
      Your flight is met at Islamabad airport and you are driven to the hotel, which is usually all anyone wants on the first day — most people arrive in the early hours after a long night in the air, and the altitude ahead is easier on a body that has slept. The afternoon is free. Islamabad is an unusual capital, laid out in the nineteen-sixties on a grid at the foot of the Margalla Hills, green and quiet compared with Lahore or Karachi, and a short drive takes you to the Faisal Mosque or up to the Daman-e-Koh viewpoint if you would rather see something than sleep.

      The working part of the day is the evening. Your guide goes through the route day by day, explains how the Skardu flight works and what happens if it does not, and answers the questions people usually have about water, toilets, washing and what the camps are actually like. Then comes the kit check, and it is worth taking seriously: boots are examined for wear, sleeping bags for temperature rating, and the gap between what you brought and what the Baltoro requires is identified now, while there are still shops. Anything missing can be bought or hired in Skardu.

      Bring your insurance certificate and the emergency number of your insurer to this briefing. We take a copy. In the event of an evacuation from the Baltoro the helicopter is not dispatched until the insurer confirms cover, and that confirmation is faster when the paperwork is already in our hands rather than in a rucksack at Concordia.

      Dinner is usually the first time the group sits together. On a trek of this length the people around that table matter: you will spend three weeks with them, and the Gondogoro crossing in particular goes better for a group that has settled into itself before the walking starts.

  - day: '02'
    title: Fly to Skardu
    description: |-
      The morning flight to Skardu is one of the great short flights anywhere. The aircraft leaves Islamabad and turns north up the Indus, and within forty minutes Nanga Parbat fills the left-hand windows — eight thousand one hundred and twenty-six metres of rock and ice rising straight out of the valley, close enough that passengers stop talking. The approach into Skardu runs between brown desert walls with the river braided below, and the landing is on a strip in a wide valley at 2,200 metres.

      It is also cancelled often. The flight is visual rather than instrument-guided through the mountains, and a layer of cloud over the range is enough to stop it. This is not a failure of planning; it is the normal condition of travel in Baltistan, and every operator here builds around it.

      If the flight does not operate, the group drives. The road is the Karakoram Highway north through the Hazara hills to the Indus, then the Skardu road that leaves the highway and climbs east beside the river through a gorge of extraordinary scale. It takes two days with a night at Chilas, and the programme absorbs both of them — this is exactly why the itinerary carries spare days at the Islamabad end. The drive is not a consolation prize. Many trekkers who have done both say the road gives a better sense of how far into the mountains you are travelling.

      Either way the day ends in Skardu, the old capital of Baltistan, sitting where the Shigar river meets the Indus with Kharpocho fort on the crag above the bazaar. The air is noticeably thinner and drier than Islamabad. Drink more water than you think you need from today; the dehydration that causes most of the headaches on the Baltoro begins here, not on the glacier.

  - day: '03'
    title: Preparation day
    description: |-
      A full day in Skardu, and a working one. Several things have to be finished before a group can start up the Braldu.

      Trekker registration comes first. Foreign trekkers are registered with the authorities in Skardu before leaving for the mountains, and the Central Karakoram National Park permit and park fee are arranged at the same time. The park fee is set each year by the Gilgit-Baltistan tourism department and is paid per trekker. We handle the paperwork; you may be asked for your passport and a photograph.

      The second task is equipment. The bazaar in Skardu has become a serious outfitting town over the last two decades, and sleeping bags, down jackets, gaiters, trekking poles, gloves and glacier glasses can all be hired or bought. The kit check in Islamabad usually produces a short list, and this is where it is filled. For the Gondogoro crossing you need a harness, a helmet and crampons; if you have not brought your own, they are issued here and fitted properly rather than handed over in a bundle.

      The third is the team. Your cook, kitchen staff and the porters for the first stage are engaged in Skardu, and loads are organised. The duffle bag you hand over is weighed — twenty-five kilograms is the normal load for one porter on this ground — and anything you will not see until the trek ends is left in storage at the hotel.

      In between there is time for Kharpocho fort, which is a stiff half-hour climb above the town for a view across the whole valley, and for the bazaar, where dried apricots and walnuts are worth buying for the glacier days. Eat well tonight. The food on trek is good, but it is not this.
    overnight: Hotel
  - day: '04'
    title: Drive to Askoli
    description: |-
      The jeep road up the Braldu is the part of the journey most trekkers remember with a particular expression. Six to seven hours from Skardu, the road leaves the cultivated Shigar valley, climbs into a defile, and narrows to a ledge cut into the cliff above the river, with a drop on one side and loose rock above on the other. Jeeps pass each other by reversing. It is safe in the sense that it is driven every day by people who have driven it for years, and alarming in every other sense.

      The valley changes character as you climb. The green of Shigar gives way to grey scree and then to a landscape of bare rock with small irrigated villages wherever a side stream allows one — Dassu, Chakpo, Hoto — each a patch of apricot and poplar in an otherwise stone-coloured world. These villages are the source of most of the porters who will carry for you.

      Askole is the last of them, and the end of the road. About eighty households live here at 3,000 metres, growing barley, wheat, potatoes and apricots on terraces fed by channels from the glacier, and keeping sheep, goats and yaks on the high pasture above. Every expedition to K2, Broad Peak, the Gasherbrums, Trango or the Biafo has started from this village for more than a century, and the villagers have grown used to it without becoming indifferent to it.

      Camp is set on the flat ground beside the village. The evening is spent weighing and distributing porter loads and registering the porters, which takes longer than you would expect and is worth watching — it is the moment the expedition stops being a plan and becomes a column of people. Last mobile signal is here, and it is patchy.
    altitude: 3,000 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '05'
    title: Trek to Jula
    description: |-
      The first day on foot, and deliberately an easy one. The route follows the Braldu river downstream of Askole on stony, undulating ground, crossing the river by a cable bridge — a flat span of planks and wire above fast grey water, taken one person at a time. It makes a good photograph and a better story.

      What surprises most people is the heat. The Braldu valley at 3,000 metres in July is a desert: bare rock, no shade, the river too silty to drink from, and the sun reflecting off pale stone from both sides. People who have packed for a glacier trek walk the first two days in a sun hat and a thin shirt and finish them with a headache from dehydration rather than altitude. Drink steadily, well past the point of thirst, and keep your arms and neck covered rather than exposed.

      Four to five hours brings the camp at Jula, on the river flat at around 3,200 metres. The camp routine begins here and will repeat for the next fortnight: the kitchen tent goes up first and tea appears within minutes of arrival, then the sleeping tents, then the mess and toilet tents. Washing water is brought in a bowl. Dinner is cooked and served in the mess tent, usually soup, then rice or chapati with lentils and vegetables, sometimes meat in the early days while it keeps.

      Afternoons at camp are the quiet pleasure of a trek like this. There is nothing to do and no signal with which to do it. Most people wash, write, sort their bag, and sleep earlier than they have in years.

  - day: '06'
    title: Trek to Paiju
    description: |-
      A longer and more interesting day, six to seven hours up the valley with the walls closing in. The trail climbs and drops across old moraine and alluvial fans, crossing side streams that run high in the afternoon with glacier melt — the reason starts are early here and will get earlier.

      Two things happen on this day that change the feel of the trek. The first is that the Trango group comes into view ahead: Great Trango and the Nameless Tower, granite walls rising more than a thousand metres clear from the glacier, among the largest cliffs on earth. The second is that the head of the valley closes with the snout of the Baltoro — not a white glacier but a wall of grey rubble several hundred metres high, dirty, cracked, and visibly moving. First-time visitors often do not recognise it as ice at all.

      Paiju sits on a terrace above the river at around 3,600 metres, below the peak of the same name. After two days of desert it is a startling place: there are willow and poplar trees, there is a spring of clean running water, and there is grass. It is the last of all three. From here to Khuspang, eight days away on the far side of the pass, you will not see a growing tree.

      The campsite is busy in July and August, with several expeditions and trekking groups in at once, and it is not always as clean as it should be. It is also sociable in a way the glacier camps are not — this is where you meet the teams heading for K2 and the Gasherbrums, hear what the pass is doing, and get the first honest report of conditions ahead.

  - day: '07'
    title: Rest day at Paiju
    description: |-
      A rest day at Paiju, and not an idle one. It is kept by every group on this route for three separate reasons.

      The first is acclimatisation. You are at 3,600 metres having gained roughly fourteen hundred metres in two days, and the body needs a day to catch up before it goes higher. The standard advice to walk high and sleep low applies: a gentle climb of two or three hours up the hillside behind camp, gaining three or four hundred metres and coming back down, does far more good than lying in a tent. It also gives the first clear view down the length of the Baltoro, which is worth the effort on its own.

      The second is logistics. The low-altitude porters bake bread here for the glacier stages, enough to last several days, using ovens dug into the ground — it takes most of the day and is interesting to watch. Loads are reorganised for the ice: what is needed daily moves to the top, and the kitchen takes on the supplies it cannot restock beyond this point.

      The third is the body in general. This is the last place with abundant clean running water, so it is where people wash themselves and their clothes properly. Everything dries within hours in that air.

      Your guide watches the group today. Headache, poor sleep and loss of appetite are common and usually harmless at this height; the same symptoms with breathlessness at rest or unsteadiness on the feet are not, and the decision about whether someone continues is better taken here, where descent is simple, than at Urdukas.
    altitude: 3,600 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '08'
    title: Trek to Khoburtze
    description: |-
      The day you step onto the Baltoro, and it is a rude introduction. The glacier snout is not a smooth tongue of ice but a chaos of rubble lying on moving ice, and the climb onto it is steep, loose and awkward, with the surface shifting underfoot and the occasional sound of stone falling into a hole you cannot see. It takes an hour or so and it is the least pleasant hour of the trek. Trekking poles earn their place here.

      Once on it, the route follows the southern margin of the glacier where the ice meets the valley wall, in and out of the ablation valley — the trough that forms along the edge where the ice has melted back. The walking alternates: sections of relatively flat moraine, then scrambles over ridges of piled rock, then stretches of bare grey ice where crampons are not needed but attention is.

      The scale is difficult to convey and takes a day or two to register. The Baltoro is sixty-two kilometres long and about two wide; the rubble on its surface is not a thin scatter but a continuous covering, and the ice beneath moves several centimetres a day. Streams run on the surface in blue channels and disappear vertically into moulins. On either side the walls go up four thousand metres.

      Khoburtse, at around 3,800 metres, is a small camp on a moraine shelf above the ice, with room for only a few tents. Water comes from a side stream. It is a quieter and often cleaner site than Paiju, and the view across to the Trango Towers from the sleeping bag at dawn is one that people keep.
    altitude: 3,800 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '09'
    title: Trek to Urdukas
    description: |-
      A short day in distance and a steep one in ascent, climbing along the southern edge of the Baltoro to Urdukas at around 4,130 metres. Four hours or so, with the glacier on your left the whole way and the valley wall rising on your right.

      Urdukas is the most famous campsite in the Karakoram and deserves the reputation. It is a series of grassy ledges set two hundred metres above the ice on a steep slope, scattered with enormous boulders that have been used as shelters for more than a century — the Duke of the Abruzzi camped here in 1909 on his way to K2, and his photographs show the same rocks. There is grass, which after Paiju is a surprise, and in July there are flowers. Ibex are sometimes seen on the slopes above.

      What it is really known for is the view. Directly opposite, across two kilometres of glacier, the Trango Towers and the Cathedral spires rise in a single wall of granite — Great Trango, the Nameless Tower, Uli Biaho, Choricho and Paiju Peak behind you. In the late afternoon the light comes round onto the faces and turns them orange, and in the hour before dark the whole group tends to stop talking and sit facing the same way.

      Two practical notes. The ledges are small and steeply banked, so tents are pitched wherever they fit and the walk to the toilet tent is an exercise in care at night. And the site is heavily used in season — it is one of the places where the pressure of traffic on the Baltoro is most visible, and where carrying out what you carry in matters most.
    altitude: 4,130 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '10'
    title: Trek to Goro-I
    description: |-
      From Urdukas the route leaves the valley edge and commits to the middle of the glacier, and the character of the trek changes completely. There is no more grass, no more moraine shelf, no more valley wall beside you. For the next four days you are walking on ice in a world made of rock, ice and sky, and nothing else.

      The walking is slower than the distance suggests. The surface of the central Baltoro is a field of low rubble-covered ridges and troughs, and the route winds between them, crossing melt channels and skirting holes. Five to six hours covers less ground than it would anywhere else. Underfoot it is stone on ice, which is tiring in a way that flat ground is not, and the glare from above and below is constant — glacier glasses from first light, not from when it starts to hurt, and sunscreen reapplied through the day including under the chin and nose.

      Masherbrum, 7,821 metres, rises to the south and dominates the afternoon. Ahead, Gasherbrum IV begins to show at the head of the valley, and the first glimpse of Mitre Peak appears, a perfect rock pyramid that marks the approach to Concordia.

      Camp at Goro I, around 4,300 metres, is pitched on the glacier itself — tents on stone and ice, with the sound of water running beneath the floor and the regular crack of the glacier moving at night. Meltwater is collected for the kitchen. It is colder than anything so far, the first night where the water bottle freezes if it is left outside, and for most people the first real test of the sleeping bag.
    altitude: 4,300 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '11'
    title: Trek to Concordia
    description: |-
      This is the day the mountain appears, and nothing else on the trek compares to it.

      The walking is five to six hours of the same slow glacier ground, steadily gaining height along the centre of the Baltoro with Gasherbrum IV growing ahead. Then the valley opens, the Godwin-Austen glacier comes in from the north, and K2 stands at the head of it — eight thousand six hundred and eleven metres, a near-perfect pyramid, apparently close enough to touch and in fact some twenty kilometres away. The scale defeats the camera. Most people stop and simply look for a while.

      Concordia, at around 4,600 metres, is the junction of the Baltoro and Godwin-Austen glaciers, named by Martin Conway in 1892 after the Place de la Concorde. Standing at the camp you can turn through a full circle and count four of the world's fourteen eight-thousand-metre peaks: K2 to the north, Broad Peak beside it, and Gasherbrum I and II to the east. Add Gasherbrum IV, Chogolisa, Mitre Peak, Marble Peak and Baltoro Kangri and there is no comparable concentration of great mountains anywhere on earth.

      The camp itself is a hard place. It sits on open ice with no shelter, the wind comes down the Godwin-Austen in the afternoon, and nights are well below freezing even in July. The toilet arrangements are basic and the ground is moving. People arrive elated and spend the evening cold.

      Take photographs in the last hour of light rather than at midday. K2 catches the sun on its south face late in the day, and from Concordia that is the view that people frame and hang on a wall.
    altitude: 4,600 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '12'
    title: Rest day at Concordia
    description: |-
      A rest day at Concordia, and the one that decides how the crossing goes.

      The medical purpose is straightforward. You are at 4,600 metres and the pass is at 5,585. A full day here, with a walk up and a return to sleep at the same height, is the difference between crossing well and crossing badly. Most groups use the morning for the walk up the Godwin-Austen towards K2 base camp — a long, flat, gradually rising glacier walk with the mountain in front of you the whole way. Reaching base camp itself and returning is a committing day of seven or eight hours; many turn around at the Broad Peak camp or at the Gilkey Memorial viewpoint and are entirely satisfied.

      The second purpose is assessment. Your guide spends this day watching the group, because tomorrow and the day after commit everyone to a 5,585-metre pass crossed in the dark on snow and ice. Appetite, sleep, breathing at rest and how people moved yesterday all feed into the decision, and so does honesty: if you have not slept and have had a headache for two days, say so today. There is no advantage in concealing it, and the guide has seen it before.

      The third thing that happens here is information. Concordia is where the Gondogoro groups, the expedition teams and the porters coming down from the pass all meet. The condition of the fixed ropes, the state of the snow on the Hushe side, whether the rope-fixing team is in place — all of it is known here, and it is the best intelligence available anywhere.

      Those who stay in camp get a day of extraordinary light moving across K2 and Broad Peak. Few people regret it.
    altitude: 4,600 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '13'
    title: Trek to Ali camp
    description: |-
      A short and deliberately gentle day, turning south away from the Baltoro and onto the Vigne glacier towards the Gondogoro. Three to four hours of walking brings the group to Ali Camp at around 5,000 metres, and almost everything about the day is arranged around what happens tonight.

      The walking itself is straightforward glacier ground, rising steadily, with Chogolisa's long white wall ahead and K2 receding behind — worth a last look back, because this is where it disappears. Mitre Peak stands over the junction. The ground is snow-covered rather than rubble by now, and the group may rope up depending on conditions.

      Ali Camp is not a comfortable place and is not meant to be. It is a flat patch of snow and moraine below the pass at the point where most parties stop before the crossing. Tents go up early, there is no shelter from wind, and at 5,000 metres almost nobody sleeps well.

      The routine is compressed. Tea and a cooked meal arrive in the late afternoon, much earlier than usual. Your guide briefs the crossing: the start time, the order of the group, where the fixed ropes begin, how to use the jumar and the descender, and what the turnaround plan is. Crampons are fitted and checked by lamplight, harnesses adjusted over the clothing you will actually wear, and everything for tomorrow is packed before you lie down — head torch, gloves, spare gloves, water somewhere it will not freeze.

      Then everyone goes to bed in the early evening. The wake-up call will come at around midnight, and the snow has to be crossed while it is still frozen hard.
    altitude: 5,000 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '14'
    title: Trek to Khuspang over Gondogoro Pass
    description: |-
      The big day, and the reason people choose this route over the Baltoro out-and-back.

      You are woken around midnight and walk out of camp by one in the morning, head torches in a line, into cold that is brutal for the first half hour. The reason for the hour is physical rather than theatrical: the snow slopes below the pass must be crossed while they are frozen, before the sun loosens them, and the descent on the far side becomes dangerous once the day warms.

      The climb to the pass takes four to six hours depending on conditions and the group. It is steep snow, and the upper section is secured with fixed ropes maintained each season — you climb using a jumar on the rope with crampons and an ice axe, and your climbing guides are on the ropes throughout. It is not technical climbing, but it is steep, exposed and at an altitude where everything takes three times as long as it should. Hands get cold on the metal. Progress is measured in ten steps and a pause.

      The top comes at 5,585 metres, usually around first light, and it is one of the great viewpoints on earth. Behind you the Baltoro peaks: K2, Broad Peak, Gasherbrum I and II, four eight-thousanders in a single line. Ahead and to the south, Masherbrum, Chogolisa and the needle of Laila Peak. There is nowhere else that four of the fourteen highest mountains in the world can be seen at once from a pass that trekkers can cross.

      Nobody stays long. The descent to Khuspang is longer and steeper than the ascent, on fixed ropes again for the upper section, then loose moraine and snow slopes for hours. It is where tired legs make mistakes, and where the day is actually won or lost. Khuspang, at around 4,650 metres, arrives in the late morning or early afternoon, and most people sleep immediately.

      The crossing is attempted only if snow conditions, weather, the state of the fixed ropes and the readiness of the group all allow it. That judgement belongs to your guide and it is final. A pass that is not in condition is not crossed, and the group returns down the Baltoro instead — which is why the itinerary is built to absorb it.
    altitude: 5,585 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '15'
    title: Free day at Khuspang or climb Gondogoro peak
    description: |-
      A day at Khuspang, below Laila Peak, and after yesterday almost everyone takes it as a rest day without argument.

      Khuspang sits at around 4,650 metres on the Hushe side of the pass, on the moraine beside the Gondogoro glacier. The setting is a complete change from the Baltoro: the peaks here are steeper and nearer, the glacier is smaller, and there is the first green on the slopes below. Laila Peak stands directly above the camp — six thousand and ninety-six metres of leaning snow blade, one of the most photographed mountains in Pakistan and one of the most difficult of its height, with a north-west face that skiers and climbers come specifically to attempt.

      Strong parties can attempt Gondogoro Peak from here, a non-technical snow summit above the camp giving a wider view back over the pass and south towards Masherbrum. It is a long day at altitude on legs that crossed a pass yesterday, and the honest answer is that most groups have one or two takers and the rest sleep, eat and sit in the sun.

      The sitting in the sun is not a waste of a day. At this height, twenty-four hours of rest after a 5,585-metre crossing does real work — appetite returns, the headache most people have carried since Concordia lifts, and the descent tomorrow is considerably more enjoyable on a body that has recovered.

      It is also the first day in a week when the afternoon is warm enough to wash properly, and the last high camp of the trek. From here the whole journey is downhill.
    altitude: 4,650 m
    overnight: Camping
  - day: '16'
    title: Trek to Shaicho
    description: |-
      The day the world turns green again, and after eleven days on rock and ice it has an effect on people that is out of proportion to the scenery.

      The route descends the Gondogoro glacier, which is small and steep compared with the Baltoro, then leaves the ice and drops into the upper Hushe valley on moraine and scree. The height loss is substantial — from 4,650 metres to around 3,400 — and it is felt as a steady increase in the thickness of the air. Breathing becomes easy again somewhere around four thousand metres and people notice the exact moment.

      The vegetation returns in stages. First moss and tiny alpines in the cracks, then juniper bushes, then willow and birch along the stream, then grass. By the time the group reaches Shaicho there are grazing animals, shepherds' huts, and the smell of vegetation and woodsmoke, which after a fortnight is almost overwhelming.

      Shaicho is a meadow camp beside the river at around 3,400 metres, with birch trees and flat grass to pitch on — the most comfortable campsite of the whole trek by a considerable margin. Washing in the river is cold but possible. The kitchen usually produces something better than usual because fresh supplies can come up from Hushe.

<h2><strong>About this journey</strong></h2><p>The Gondogoro La circuit takes everything the K2 base camp trek offers and adds the finest viewpoint in the Karakoram. The route in is the same &mdash; Askole, the Baltoro glacier, Urdukas, Concordia and the K2 base camp area &mdash; but instead of retracing its steps down the glacier, the group turns south and crosses the Gondogoro La at 5,585 metres into the Hushe valley.</p><p>That one decision changes the character of the whole trek. Instead of walking out the way you walked in, you finish in a different valley, among different people, having stood on a pass from which four of the world&rsquo;s fourteen eight-thousand-metre peaks are visible at once: K2, Broad Peak, Gasherbrum I and Gasherbrum II, with Masherbrum, Chogolisa and Laila Peak on the southern side. There is no other trekking pass on earth with that view.</p><p>It is also the harder choice. The crossing is a pre-dawn climb on snow and ice using fixed ropes, a harness and crampons, and it is attempted only when conditions and the group allow. Everything in this programme &mdash; the rest days, the altitude profile, the spare days at the end &mdash; is arranged around giving that crossing the best possible chance.</p><h2><strong>Who this trek is for</strong></h2><p>Strong, experienced hill walkers who want more than an out-and-back route and are comfortable with three weeks away from roads, shops, hotels and mobile signal. You do not need to be a climber. You do need to be able to walk six to eight hours a day on rough ground, day after day, carrying a daypack, and to keep doing it at four and a half thousand metres when you have not slept properly for a week.</p><p>Previous glacier experience helps and is not essential; our climbing guides fix and manage the ropes on the pass and talk every member of the group through the technique at Ali Camp the evening before. What matters more is honest fitness and a steady head on steep ground. If exposure frightens you &mdash; genuinely frightens you, rather than merely concentrates you &mdash; the descent from the pass will be a long and unhappy morning.</p><p><strong>Who it is not for.</strong> Anyone looking for a first Himalayan trek, anyone who needs a shower and a bed, and anyone whose fitness is theoretical. It is also not the right choice for travellers on a fixed return date with no margin: the Skardu flight and the pass both answer to the weather, and this itinerary needs the spare days it carries. If you want the Baltoro and Concordia without the pass, the K2 base camp trek covers the same glacier and returns the way it came.</p><h2><strong>The Baltoro glacier and Concordia</strong></h2><p>Before the pass there is the glacier, and the glacier is half the reason to come.</p><p>The Baltoro runs some sixty kilometres from Concordia down to its snout near Jula, a river of ice up to a kilometre wide, buried under rubble for most of its length and moving a few centimetres a day. Walking on it is nothing like walking on snow. The surface is grey moraine over ice, pitted with meltwater channels, sinkholes and blue pools, cracking and shifting audibly in the afternoon heat. Routes change from season to season and sometimes from week to week, which is why local guides matter more here than any map.</p><p>Above it stand the Trango Towers and the Great Trango, among the largest granite walls on earth; Uli Biaho; the Cathedral Spires; Mustagh Tower, so improbably shaped that early surveyors doubted it could be climbed; and Masherbrum to the south. The famous first view of Paiju Peak comes early, and the walls close in from Urdukas onward.</p><p><strong>Concordia</strong> is where the Baltoro meets the Godwin-Austen and Vigne glaciers, and it is one of the great amphitheatres of the world. From a single patch of moraine at 4,600 metres you can see K2 to the north, Broad Peak and the Gasherbrums to the east, and Chogolisa and Mitre Peak to the south. Four eight-thousanders are within view. The mountaineer who named it after the Place de la Concorde was not exaggerating by much. Our itinerary gives it a full rest day, and that day is the one most people remember.</p><h2><strong>The Gondogoro La</strong></h2><p>The pass deserves its own description, because it is the reason this trek exists and the part people ask about most.</p><p>It sits at 5,585 metres on the ridge between the Vigne glacier, which runs south from Concordia, and the Gondogoro glacier, which runs down into the Hushe valley. Shepherds and hunters have used it for centuries. As a trekking route it was opened to groups in the 1980s and is now maintained each season with fixed ropes on both the northern and southern sides by a rope-fixing team, usually from Hushe.</p><p>The crossing begins from Ali Camp at about 5,000 metres, with a wake-up call around midnight and a start by one in the morning. The timing is not for effect. The snow slopes below the pass have to be crossed while they are frozen hard; once the sun is on them the surface softens, the risk of slides rises, and the long descent on the southern side becomes materially more dangerous. Groups that leave late have to turn around.</p><p>The ascent takes four to six hours in the dark on steep snow, with the final section secured by fixed rope &mdash; you ascend using a jumar clipped to the rope, with crampons on and an ice axe in the other hand. It is not technical climbing by any mountaineering standard. It is, however, steep, cold, exposed and at an altitude where a hundred metres of height gain can take half an hour. Hands suffer on the metal; two pairs of gloves is not excessive.</p><p>The top is usually reached around first light, and nobody lingers long. The descent to Khuspang is longer and steeper than the climb, again on fixed ropes for the upper part, then several hours of loose moraine and snow. Most groups are in camp by early afternoon, and most people sleep for the rest of it.</p><h2><strong>If the pass is closed</strong></h2><p>The crossing is never guaranteed. Heavy new snow, poor visibility, damaged or missing fixed ropes, or a group that has not acclimatised well are all reasons to turn back, and the decision belongs to your guide on the day.</p><p>When the pass is not crossed, the group returns from Concordia down the Baltoro by the way it came in, and exits via Askole and Skardu. The trek is not cancelled and the days are not wasted &mdash; it becomes the K2 base camp trek, which is a magnificent journey in its own right and the reason most trekkers come to Pakistan at all. The itinerary is built with enough spare time to absorb the change without missing international flights.</p><p>We say this plainly at the time of booking rather than in the small print, because a group that understands the possibility in advance makes a better decision on the mountain than one that feels it has paid for a guarantee.</p><h2><strong>Hushe, where the trek ends</strong></h2><p>Most descriptions of this route stop at the pass. The southern side deserves better.</p><p>Below Khuspang the Gondogoro glacier runs down into the Hushe valley, and the change is abrupt: after two weeks of rock, ice and grey moraine, there is grass. Dalsampa and Saicho are summer pastures where Hushe families bring animals up for the season, and they are often the first green and the first smell of woodsmoke in a fortnight.</p><p><strong>Laila Peak</strong> stands over the walk out, a near-perfect leaning spearhead of 6,096 metres that is among the most photographed mountains in Pakistan and still rarely climbed. The valley below it is Balti farmland &mdash; stone-walled terraces of barley and potato, apricot and walnut trees, irrigation channels cut from the glacier melt, flat-roofed houses stacked along the slope.</p><p><strong>Hushe village</strong> itself, at around 3,050 metres, is the roadhead and the last settlement in the valley. It is a mountaineering village in the proper sense: for decades the high-altitude porters and climbers who have worked on K2, the Gasherbrums and Masherbrum have come from here, and several of the best-known Pakistani mountaineers were born within a few hundred metres of the road&rsquo;s end. The men who fix the ropes on the Gondogoro La each season are usually from this village. Arriving on foot from the north, having crossed the pass they maintain, is a better introduction to the place than any amount of reading.</p><h2><strong>When to go</strong></h2><p>The season for this route is short. <strong>Mid-July to late August</strong> is when the Gondogoro La is most likely to be in condition, and that is when we run it.</p><p><strong>June</strong> is usually too early. The Baltoro is walkable but the pass often still carries deep unconsolidated snow and the ropes may not be fixed for the season. <strong>Early July</strong> can work in a light winter and is a gamble in a heavy one.</p><p><strong>Mid-July to mid-August</strong> is the main window. The snow has consolidated, the ropes are in, the rope-fixing team is on the ground, and the weather is at its most settled. Very little of the Baltoro is a path. It is moraine, glacier ice, loose rubble on moving ice, boulder fields and stream crossings, and it is tiring in a way that a hill path at home is not.
