faqs:
  - question: How hard is the K2 base camp trek?
    answer: >-
      It is a demanding trek rather than a climb. There is no technical climbing, but you walk six
      to eight hours a day for about two weeks, much of it on the rough moraine of the Baltoro
      Glacier, and you sleep above 4,000m for several nights. Regular hill walking with a loaded
      pack for three to four months beforehand is the best preparation.
  - question: When is the best time for the K2 base camp trek?
    answer: >-
      Late June to early September. July and August are the most settled, which is also when the
      expedition teams are on the Baltoro, so the glacier feels busy and alive. Shoulder dates can
      bring snow on the higher camps.
  - question: Do I need a permit for the Baltoro and Concordia?
    answer: >-
      Yes. The route runs through Central Karakoram National Park, so park fees and trekking
      paperwork apply, and foreign trekkers are registered before leaving Skardu. We handle the
      permits, registration and the Askole road arrangements.

  - day: "04"
    title: Drive to Askole
    description: >-
      A long, rough and genuinely memorable day in four-wheel drives. The road leaves Skardu along
      the Shigar valley through apricot orchards and poplar windbreaks, past Shigar's old wooden
      fort, then turns north into the Braldu gorge and stops pretending to be a road. What follows
      is six to eight hours of track cut into the side of a canyon, in places a single vehicle wide,
      in places with a drop of several hundred metres on the downhill side and no barrier at all.
      Drivers here are very good and have done this for years. Passengers tend to be quiet. The
      gorge is a geology lesson at full scale: folded and tilted strata, dry side-valleys that flood
      without warning in July, and the Braldu river below running grey-white with glacial flour at a
      volume that makes conversation difficult. Askole, at 3,000 metres, is the last village in the
      valley and the end of the road in every sense. Perhaps two hundred stone and mud houses,
      flat-roofed, terraced fields of barley and potato watered by channels cut from the glacier,
      apricot and walnut trees, and a population that has supplied porters to Karakoram expeditions
      since the Duke of the Abruzzi came through in 1909. Every trek and every expedition on the
      Baltoro starts here. Camp goes up outside the village. The afternoon and evening belong to
      logistics: loads are weighed and distributed, the porter team is assembled and recorded, and
      your guide settles the plan for tomorrow. Walk into the village, drink tea if you are offered
      it, and go to bed early. The walking starts in the morning.

  - day: "05"
    title: Trek to Jhola
    description: >-
      The first day on foot, and a kind introduction by Karakoram standards: six to seven hours on a
      reasonable trail with little height gained. The path leaves Askole's fields and drops onto the
      broad gravel flats of the Braldu, where the river braids across a plain several hundred metres
      wide. Within two hours the snout of the Biafo glacier comes into view on the left, a filthy
      grey wall of ice and rubble pushing into the valley from the north. The Biafo runs for more
      than sixty kilometres up to Snow Lake and the Hispar pass, part of one of the longest glacier
      systems outside the poles, and you walk past the end of it as a sideshow on the way to
      something else. That is the scale of this valley. Beyond Biafo the path crosses the Dumordo
      river. Depending on the season and the water this is done by a jeep bridge, a cable trolley,
      or a wade with the porters, and your guide decides on the day. Camp is at Jhola, around 3,200
      metres, on sand and gravel above the river. The walking today is easy enough that people talk,
      and that matters: you will spend two weeks with this group and today is when it becomes one.
      It is also the day to get your systems right. Learn where things live in your daypack. Drink
      more than you want to. Deal with the first hot spot on your heel immediately rather than
      tonight, because a blister at Jhola is a nuisance and the same blister at Urdukas is a serious
      problem with no road out.

  - day: "06"
    title: Trek to Paiju
    description: >-
      Six to seven hours up the Braldu valley to Paiju, and the day the mountains stop being
      distant. The path stays close to the river on loose grey ground, crossing fans of debris where
      side-streams have dumped their load, with the valley walls narrowing and rising on both sides.
      There is almost no shade and the ground reflects heat upward; this is one of the warmer days
      of the trek and people regularly underestimate how much water it takes. Then, perhaps three
      hours in, Paiju Peak appears ahead and to the right, and the rest of the day is spent walking
      towards it. At 6,610 metres it is not among the giants of this range, but it is close, steep
      and beautifully proportioned, and because it stands at the gate of the Baltoro it is one of
      the most photographed peaks in Pakistan. Behind and above it, the first slim granite needles
      of the Trango group begin to show. Paiju camp sits at about 3,600 metres on a shoulder below
      the peak. After a day on bare grey gravel it feels almost absurdly hospitable: willows, a cold
      spring that genuinely runs clear, and ground soft enough that you do not feel every stone
      through the mat. Make the most of that tonight, because tomorrow the route steps onto the
      Baltoro and does not step off it again for eight days. The camp is busy in season, shared with
      expedition teams heading for K2, Broad Peak and the Gasherbrums. Porter groups cluster around
      their own fires. The evening here, with the Trango spires catching the last light above the
      camp, is the first of this trek's great evenings.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 3,600 m
  - day: "07"
    title: Paiju acclimatization
    description: >-
      A rest day at Paiju, and the most important day of the first week. It is not a reward and it
      is not padding. You have come from 500 metres to 3,600 in five days. The body's response to
      thin air is to make more red blood cells, and that takes time it has not yet had. Spending a
      full day and two nights at this height is what makes Concordia possible later, and groups that
      skip it pay for it at Goro. Do not spend the day lying in a tent. Your guide takes anyone who
      wants it on a short walk a few hundred metres up the slope behind camp, which is the correct
      way to use a rest day: climb a little, come back down, sleep low. The view from up there takes
      in the Baltoro snout and the whole gate of the glacier. Paiju is also the day the expedition
      reorganises itself. Loads are checked and redistributed, bread is baked for the days ahead,
      and the porters prepare for glacier travel, which is harder on them than anything behind us.
      Traditionally this is the day an animal is slaughtered and the porters eat properly before the
      ice; it is worth understanding that this is a working camp for several dozen men, not only a
      campsite for you. Wash today, in the spring, because this is the last running water for a
      week. Dry your boots. Air your sleeping bag. Then your guide gives the glacier briefing: how
      to move on moraine, how to follow the route exactly, and why the person in front of you is
      choosing their line.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 3,600 m
  - day: "08"
    title: Trek to Khoburtse
    description: >-
      The day you step onto the Baltoro, and the day the trek changes character completely. Six to
      seven hours. An hour from camp the path reaches the snout of the glacier, a two-hundred-metre
      wall of dirty ice with the Braldu river bursting out from underneath it in a cave of blue.
      That river you have followed for three days is born here, from this ice. From this point there
      is no path. The surface of the Baltoro is not ice you can see. It is grey moraine, boulders
      and rubble lying on top of moving ice, and the walking is unlike anything most people have
      done: up and over the debris, down into troughs, across meltwater channels, around sinkholes
      with blue water at the bottom. Nothing is level and nothing is stable. Ankles work harder than
      lungs. Your guide's exact line matters here, because what was solid last season may be a hole
      this one. Above and to the left, the Trango group now stands clear, and the Nameless Tower in
      particular: a single blade of granite rising more than a thousand metres above its glacier,
      one of the most coveted big walls on earth. Below it the Great Trango throws down a face that
      climbers have measured in vertical kilometres. You walk beneath them for most of the day. Camp
      is at Khoburtse, around 3,930 metres, on moraine beside the glacier's edge. There is a little
      grass and a little shelter here, which is why the name means roughly the green and dry place.
      From tomorrow there is neither.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 3,930 m
  - day: "09"
    title: Trek to Urdukas
    description: >-
      A shorter day, four to five hours, and the finest campsite on the Baltoro. The route continues
      along the lateral moraine and then climbs onto the ablation valley on the true left bank, the
      strip of ground between the glacier's edge and the mountainside. The walking is slightly
      kinder than yesterday and the views are extraordinary in every direction, which makes for slow
      progress and a great many photographs. Urdukas sits at about 4,200 metres on a steep grassy
      shoulder perhaps a hundred metres above the ice, and after two days on grey rubble the grass
      alone is worth the climb. The tents go on stone-built terraces that generations of expeditions
      have cut into the slope. There is water, there are wildflowers in July, and there are ibex on
      the crags above if you watch for long enough. The reason to come here, though, is the view
      across the glacier. Directly opposite stands the whole Trango massif with Uli Biaho and the
      Cathedral Spires beside it, and the light moves across those walls all afternoon and evening.
      Behind camp, to the south, Masherbrum rises to 7,821 metres, the peak the surveyors first
      labelled K1 before they settled their numbering on its neighbour. This is also where altitude
      starts to be noticeable for most people: shorter breath on the climb into camp, a headache in
      the evening, broken sleep. That is normal and expected at this height. Tell your guide anyway.
      He asks every evening for a reason, and a straight answer is more useful than a brave one.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,200 m
  - day: "10"
    title: Trek to Goro II
    description: >-
      A long and serious day on the ice, six to seven hours, and the one most people find hardest so
      far. The route drops off the Urdukas shoulder and goes back onto the glacier, and this time it
      stays in the middle of it. The Baltoro here is more than a kilometre wide, and the walking is
      relentless: moraine ridges to cross one after another, loose rock on ice, no shade, no shelter
      and nowhere flat. The altitude is now high enough that the pace feels slow even when it is
      not, and the day is as much about patience as fitness. What you get in return is the central
      Karakoram with nothing in the way. Mustagh Tower appears ahead, 7,276 metres, so improbably
      sheer that the surveyors who first drew it were accused of exaggeration and climbers left it
      alone until 1956. Masherbrum holds the southern skyline. Ahead, the valley opens and the first
      eight-thousanders begin to show themselves above the glacier's far end. Camp at Goro II,
      around 4,500 metres, is pitched directly on the glacier: tents on flattened moraine over ice,
      with nothing growing and nothing living. It is cold as soon as the sun goes, and it is where
      most trekkers first find out how good their sleeping bag really is. Put your water bottle in
      the bag with you. Many groups consider the evening at Goro the most atmospheric of the trek.
      K2 is not yet visible. Broad Peak is, and it is close enough now that you can see the route up
      it.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,500 m
  - day: "11"
    title: Reach Concordia
    description: >-
      Concordia, and the reason people come to Pakistan. It is a short day by the standards of this
      trek, four to five hours, and the group tends to walk it quickly. The glacier bends
      north-east, the valley widens, and then the route reaches the place where the Baltoro meets
      the Godwin-Austen glacier coming down from the north and the Vigne coming in from the south,
      and suddenly there is nothing in front of you at all. K2 stands at the head of the
      Godwin-Austen, fifteen kilometres away and eight and a half vertical kilometres high, and it
      does not look like a mountain so much as an argument. A near-perfect pyramid of rock and ice,
      steeper than anything around it, with a plume of spindrift off the summit ridge most
      afternoons. At 8,611 metres it is the second highest mountain on earth and, by common
      agreement among the people who climb such things, the hardest of the eight-thousanders. Of the
      fourteen peaks above eight thousand metres, only Annapurna has killed a greater share of those
      who attempted it. Turn clockwise from it and you have Broad Peak at 8,051 metres, so close
      that its three summits are distinct; Gasherbrum IV, whose west face is among the most
      celebrated walls in mountaineering; Gasherbrum I at 8,080 metres and Gasherbrum II at 8,035;
      then Chogolisa, Mitre Peak, and the long southern wall above the Vigne. Four eight-thousanders
      from one patch of moraine at 4,600 metres. Nowhere else on earth puts that many of them in a
      single field of view. The name was borrowed from the Place de la Concorde by a survey party
      who found the comparison obvious; standing on the moraine at 4,600 metres, it is difficult to
      argue with them.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,600 m
  - day: "12"
    title: Trek to K2 base-camp excursion
    description: >-
      The day you walk to the foot of K2, and the longest day of the trek. Expect a start well
      before dawn and ten to fourteen hours on the move, and expect to be very tired at the end of
      it. The route turns north up the Godwin-Austen glacier, named for the surveyor who mapped this
      country in 1861, with K2 directly ahead the entire way. It does not appear to get closer for
      the first three hours, which tells you more about the scale of the Karakoram than any figure
      can. You pass Broad Peak base camp, usually a small town of tents in July and August, and the
      hum of a working expedition: loads going up, climbers coming down, the kitchen generators, the
      laundry. Then, on a rock promontory below K2, there is the Gilkey Memorial. Art Gilkey died on
      this mountain in 1953 and his companions could not bring him down. The cairn raised for him
      now carries plates, plaques and engraved mess tins for the many who have died here since, and
      reading them is the single most sobering quarter of an hour on the Baltoro. People go quiet.
      K2 base camp lies at about 5,000 metres on the glacier under the Abruzzi Spur, the ridge the
      Duke of the Abruzzi reconnoitred in 1909 and the Italians finally climbed on 31 July 1954.
      From here the mountain is directly overhead and foreshortened into something that barely looks
      possible. Then you turn around and walk back to Concordia. Whether the full excursion runs
      depends on the day, the weather and the group, and your guide decides.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 5,000 m  (Concordia 4,600 m)
  - day: "13"
    title: Concordia recovery
    description: >-
      A day at Concordia with no distance to cover, held in the itinerary for weather, recovery, or
      both. If the last two days have cost you, this is when you get them back: eat, drink, sleep in
      the afternoon, and let a body that has been working at four and a half thousand metres catch
      up. If you are strong and the weather is clear, it is one of the best days of the trek to
      simply be here. Concordia rewards sitting still. The light moves around the amphitheatre all
      day and the mountains look like different mountains at six in the morning, at noon and at
      seven in the evening. K2 usually clouds over by late morning and clears again towards sunset,
      which is when it is at its most astonishing. Chogolisa, 7,665 metres on the southern skyline,
      is where Hermann Buhl died in 1957, and Mitre Peak beside it gives the one classic photograph
      that puts all four eight-thousanders in a single frame. There is usually an option for a short
      walk a little way up the Vigne glacier towards the south, or towards Broad Peak base camp if
      yesterday's excursion was shortened by weather, and your guide will offer whatever the
      conditions allow. It is also the day of the reckoning, honestly made. Your guide reviews how
      everyone has handled the altitude, because from tomorrow the trek turns around and goes down,
      and down is what the body has been waiting for. Nobody has ever regretted a day at Concordia.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,600 m
  - day: "14"
    title: Return toward Goro
    description: >-
      The trek turns around today, and the walk back down the Baltoro is not the same walk in
      reverse. Five to six hours to Goro. The first thing you notice is your own breathing. Losing
      height at this altitude is a perceptible gift: the legs work, the lungs are not rationing, and
      the pace that felt punishing on the way up now feels ordinary. Most people cover ground faster
      going down the glacier than they did coming up it, and arrive in camp wondering why this was
      ever hard. The second thing you notice is that you are now looking the other way. Coming up,
      the Trango spires and Mustagh Tower were ahead of you and the eight-thousanders were a reward
      at the end. Going down, the giants are behind you and the view in front is the long grey river
      of ice you have just spent a week on, bending away west between walls that now look familiar.
      People who were too tired to look up at Goro on the way in tend to stop constantly on the way
      out. Turn around often today. K2 is visible from the glacier for part of the morning and then
      it is not, and most trekkers do not notice the last time they see it until some hours
      afterwards. Give it a proper look while it is there. Camp is back at Goro II on the ice, and
      the second night here is colder in memory and easier in practice, because the body has
      adjusted and the altitude is now behind you rather than ahead.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,500 m
  - day: "15"
    title: Trek to Urdukas
    description: >-
      Back down the glacier to Urdukas, five to six hours, and the day the trek becomes comfortable.
      The route retraces the central moraine and then climbs back onto the ablation valley and the
      grassy shoulder. The ground is the same difficult rubble it was a week ago and it is
      noticeably easier, which has nothing to do with the ground: six days ago you were
      acclimatising and climbing, and today you are acclimatised and descending. This is the day
      most trekkers realise how much stronger they have become. Urdukas in the afternoon sun, with
      grass underfoot and water running, is one of the simple pleasures of this route. Wash
      something. Lie down on something soft. There is still ibex on the crags, there are still
      flowers in the ablation valley, and the Trango walls opposite are still the best seat in the
      Karakoram. This is also a good afternoon to watch the porters, who have carried your camp up
      this glacier and are now carrying it down. They move at a speed that is faintly insulting to
      anyone who has spent two weeks feeling fit, in sandals or worn boots, with twenty-five
      kilograms on a rope over the shoulder, and they do it as a job in a short season. If you take
      one set of photographs home that is not a mountain, make it this one, and ask first. Appetite
      usually returns tonight. At Concordia most people pick at dinner. At Urdukas they finish it
      and ask what else there is.
    overnight: Camping
    altitude: 4,200 m
  - day: "16"
    title: Trek to Paiju
    description: >-
      Off the glacier and back to Paiju, five to six hours, and a day the senses do most of the
      reporting. The route follows the moraine down past Khoburtse and then reaches the snout, where
      the Braldu comes roaring out of the ice cave, and you step off the Baltoro onto ground that
      does not move. After eight days it is a genuinely strange sensation: solid earth feels wrong
      for the first hour, and then it feels extremely good. What follows is the return of everything

<h2><strong>About this journey</strong></h2><p>Twenty-one days from Islamabad to the foot of K2 and back, on foot for sixteen of them, almost entirely on the Baltoro glacier. It is the most famous trek in Pakistan and one of the few genuinely great walks left anywhere that is not organised around lodges, shops and signposts.</p><p>The route goes in by jeep to Askole, the last village in the Braldu valley, and then walks: up the river flats to Paiju, onto the ice below the Trango Towers, along the moraine to Urdukas and Goro, and into Concordia at 4,600 metres, the junction where three great glaciers converge and four separate eight-thousand-metre peaks stand in a single field of view. From there a long day goes north under Broad Peak to K2 base camp at about 5,000 metres. Then the whole thing is walked back down.</p><p>There is no pass to cross and no technical climbing. You do not need ropes, crampons or any mountaineering skill. What you do need is the fitness and the temperament for two weeks of six-to-eight-hour days on broken glacial rubble, sleeping in a tent every night, with no road, shop, lodge or mobile signal between Askole and Askole.</p><p>What you get for that is the Karakoram at full scale, and a view of the second highest mountain on earth from close enough that you can see the route up it.</p><h2><strong>K2: the mountain you are walking towards</strong></h2><p>It is worth knowing something about K2 before you stand underneath it, because the mountain has a history that very few others can match and the base camp is where most of it happened.</p><p><strong>The name.</strong> In 1856 a British survey officer, Thomas Montgomerie, was working his way through the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India from a station in Kashmir, more than two hundred kilometres away. He sketched the two most prominent peaks on the Karakoram horizon and labelled them K1 and K2, the K standing for the range. K1 turned out to be a mountain the Balti people already had a name for, Masherbrum, and the label was dropped. K2 did not, because it cannot be seen from any inhabited place: it stands behind a wall of other mountains at the head of a glacier system five days&rsquo; walk from the last village. A surveyor&rsquo;s filing reference therefore became the permanent name of the second highest mountain on earth, which is very nearly unique. Chhogo Ri, Balti for roughly &lsquo;the big mountain&rsquo;, is sometimes offered as a local name, and it is better understood as an accurate description than as a name in long-standing use.</p><p><strong>The attempts.</strong> The first serious party came in 1902 and spent sixty-eight days on the glacier getting to about 6,500 metres on the north-east ridge. In 1909 Prince Luigi Amedeo, Duke of the Abruzzi, arrived with the photographer Vittorio Sella, reconnoitred the south-east ridge, decided it was the only plausible line, and got no higher than 6,250 metres. That ridge still carries his name &mdash; the Abruzzi Spur &mdash; and is the route almost every ascent has used since. Sella&rsquo;s photographs of the Baltoro are still reproduced today; you will pass the places he set his plate camera up.</p><p>American expeditions came in 1938, 1939 and 1953. The 1939 party got to roughly 8,380 metres and then lost four men on the descent. The 1953 party lost Art Gilkey. One of its members, George Bell, came home and described K2 as a savage mountain that tries to kill you, and the phrase has followed it ever since.</p><p><strong>The first ascent</strong> came on 31 July 1954, when an Italian expedition under Ardito Desio put Achille Compagnoni and Lino Lacedelli on the summit. They got there partly because Walter Bonatti and a Hunza porter named Amir Mehdi carried oxygen bottles to roughly 8,100 metres and then survived a night in the open with no tent. Mehdi lost his toes to frostbite. Whether the two summit climbers behaved honourably towards them was argued about in Italy for the next fifty years.</p><p><strong>And in winter</strong>, nothing. Of the fourteen mountains above eight thousand metres, K2 was the last to be climbed in winter, and it stayed unclimbed in that season until a team of ten Nepali climbers reached the summit together on 16 January 2021. Among the eight-thousanders, only Annapurna has killed a greater proportion of those who have tried it.</p><h2><strong>What you actually see from base camp</strong></h2><p>Base camp sits on the Godwin-Austen glacier at around 5,000 metres, which means the mountain above you rises another three and a half vertical kilometres. That is more vertical relief in one view than almost anywhere on the planet.</p><p>From the moraine you can follow the Abruzzi Spur with your eye from the glacier upward: the lower rock buttress, the steep chimney section that the 1938 expedition found a way through, the dark band of broken rock above it, the long shoulder, and then the summit pyramid foreshortened into something that looks far too steep to walk on. Above and left of the final section hangs the ice. Everyone who stands here looks at it for a while.</p><p>What strikes most trekkers is not the height but the steepness. The eight-thousanders of Nepal rise out of a crowd of other big mountains. K2 does not: it stands alone at the head of its glacier, and from directly beneath it the thing is a spike. Photographs do not convey it. Standing on the ice at its foot does.</p><p>Broad Peak base camp is passed on the way, usually full of tents in July and August, and the comings and goings of a working expedition are part of the day. If you want the climbing side of this mountain rather than the walking side, our <a href="/expeditions/k2-expedition/">K2 expedition programme</a> runs from the same base camp.</p><h2><strong>The Gilkey Memorial</strong></h2><p>A short way below K2 base camp, on a rock promontory above the glacier, there is a cairn. Almost every trekker who comes here finds it the most affecting fifteen minutes of the trip, and it is worth knowing what it is before you reach it.</p><p>In August 1953 an American expedition was high on the Abruzzi Spur when Art Gilkey, a geologist from Iowa, developed a blood clot in his leg and could not walk. His companions wrapped him and tried to lower him down the mountain in appalling weather over several days, an attempt that very nearly killed all of them. On the fourth day he was swept away by an avalanche while they were anchoring the ropes. The team built a cairn for him on the way out, and his remains were not found until 1993.</p><p>The cairn is still there, and it is no longer only his. It now carries engraved plates, metal plaques and flattened mess tins bearing the names of people who have died on K2 and on Broad Peak in the seventy years since, in several languages, some professionally cut and some scratched by hand. Prayer flags are strung above it.</p><p>There is no interpretation board and no fence. People read the names, and then they are quiet for most of the walk back. It is the part of this trek that trekkers mention years later, more often than the mountain.</p><h2><strong>Askole, and the valley the walk begins in</strong></h2><p>The Baltoro has been written about a great deal. The valley you drive up to reach it has not, and it deserves better.</p><p>The Braldu is a deep, dry, difficult gorge, and the road into it is cut from the canyon wall. Askole, at 3,000 metres, is the last village on it: a few hundred people, stone and mud houses with flat roofs, terraced fields of barley and potato on every patch of ground that can be levelled, apricot and walnut trees, and irrigation channels cut by hand to bring glacier meltwater across the slope. The Balti people here speak a Tibetan language, farm at an altitude where the growing season lasts a few months, and have done both for a very long time.</p><p>Men from Askole have worked on Karakoram expeditions since 1909, when the Abruzzi party recruited here on its way towards K2. More or less every expedition to K2, Broad Peak, the Gasherbrums and the Trangos, and every trekking group on the Baltoro, has started from this village and employed its men. In a valley with no other industry and a short season, that work matters enormously, and the arrangements around it &mdash; who is hired, in what order, at what rate &mdash; are taken seriously by everyone involved.</p><p>You will spend an afternoon and an evening here at the start and, usually, an hour at the end. Walk into the village, accept tea if it is offered, and ask before photographing anyone, particularly women. The children will talk to you. Beyond Askole, everything you eat, sleep under and drink from is carried on somebody&rsquo;s back from this point.</p><h2><strong>The men who carry the camp</strong></h2><p>This trek is made possible by porters, and travelling with a reasonable understanding of their work changes how the fortnight feels.</p><p>A porter carries 25 kilograms, which is the standard load on this route. Your own duffle bag can weigh up to that amount and one man carries it, along with his own food and bedding; you walk with a daypack holding water, a jacket and a camera. They are paid at the rates set by the Gilgit-Baltistan authorities, and above those rates when the ground or the season warrants it. In a group of a dozen trekkers the porter team is substantial, and the camp you sleep in each night exists because forty or fifty loads walked there.</p><p>They are also, as a rule, faster than you. A line of porters will pass the group on the worst moraine of the day, in sandals or well-used boots, with a sack on a rope over one shoulder, and be at the campsite with the tea on by the time you arrive. This is not showing off; it is a job in a season that lasts ten weeks.</p><p>Porters in this region do not usually hold insurance of their own. Karakoram Vision Pakistan insures every porter and every member of staff through Alpha Insurance before a trek leaves Skardu, arranged and paid for by us. We mention it plainly because it is not universal in this industry and we think it should be.</p><p>Tips are customary, not fixed, and the usual practice is for the group to agree a figure with the guide towards the end and hand it over at Askole. Nobody will put a number to you in advance. If you want to photograph the porters, and you should, ask first.</p><h2><strong>Who this trek suits, and who it does not</strong></h2><p><strong>It suits</strong> fit, patient walkers who want a wilderness trek rather than a sightseeing holiday, and who are untroubled by two weeks without a shower, a bed, a shop or a phone. No climbing experience is needed. Experience of multi-day walking is close to essential, because the difficulty here is cumulative: any single day of this route is manageable, and it is the fourteenth consecutive one at altitude that finds people out.</p><p><strong>It does not suit</strong> anyone whose fitness is aspirational rather than actual, anyone who needs certainty about dates, and anyone who would find the absence of an exit distressing. From Urdukas onwards, the way out is to walk out, which takes days. That is precisely what makes it worth doing, and it is not for everybody.</p><p><strong>It is also not a technical route</strong>, and this is where people are sometimes misled by the name. Walking to K2 base camp has nothing in common with climbing K2 beyond the first morning&rsquo;s view. There are no ropes, no crampons for the standard route in normal conditions, and no mountaineering skills required. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise, and do not assume it is therefore easy; a long glacier trek at four thousand metres is hard in its own ordinary way.</p><p>On age: we have taken trekkers in their sixties to Concordia and turned back fit twenty-five-year-olds at Goro. Altitude does not consult your birth certificate. Honest hill fitness and a willingness to go slowly matter far more than how old you are.</p><h2><strong>This trek, or the Gondogoro La?</strong></h2><p>These two are our most-asked-about programmes and they share their first eleven days exactly, so the question comes up constantly. The trail is a well-trodden path rather than moraine. If something goes wrong there is a village with a clinic within a few hours, and the route is busy enough that help is close. It is also a cultural journey through Sherpa country, which this trek is not.</p><p><strong>This trek is harder, longer and wilder.</strong> Twenty-one days, camping throughout, around 5,000 metres at its highest point, no lodges, no villages after the fourth day, no signal, and eight days on the surface of a glacier. The ground is far rougher. The commitment is of a different order.</p><p><strong>On mountains, though, the comparison is not close.</strong> Everest base camp does not have a good view of Everest; trekkers climb Kala Patthar to see it properly. It is also quieter and cooler for the walking.</p><p><strong>July and August</strong> are the main season. The weather is at its most reliable, the glacier route is established and well travelled, and every expedition on the Baltoro is in place, which many trekkers find adds to the experience rather than detracting from it. Days on the lower valley are hot &mdash; the mid-twenties Celsius in full sun, hotter on the reflective gravel &mdash; and nights at Concordia are below freezing in every month of the season.</p><p><strong>Late August into early September</strong> brings the quietest camps and, often, the clearest air and the best photography of the whole season, with a steadily increasing chance of an early snowfall and an unsettled spell.</p><p>Outside those months this route is not a trekking proposition. The Braldu road closes, the camps are under snow, and Skardu flights are unreliable for weeks at a time.</p><h2><strong>Altitude, and how this itinerary handles it</strong></h2><p>Altitude is the single thing most likely to end a trek here, and almost everything about the shape of these twenty-one days is a response to it.</p><p>The profile goes from Islamabad at 500 metres to Skardu at 2,228, Askole at 3,000, Paiju at 3,600 with a full rest day, then onto the glacier to 3,930, 4,200 and 4,500 metres on successive days, Concordia at 4,600 with a rest day, and base camp at about 5,150 before coming down. Sleeping altitude rises by around three hundred metres a day across the glacier section, which is a conservative rate, and the two rest days are placed exactly where they do the most good.</p><p>Those rest days are not negotiable and they are not spare time. A group that pushes through Paiju to save a day will have somebody in trouble at Goro, and somebody in trouble at Goro costs far more than a day.</p><p>Expect, and do not be alarmed by: headache in the evenings, poor and broken sleep, vivid dreams, no appetite, and shortness of breath on any climb. Nearly everyone gets some of this above 4,000 metres. The honest answer is the useful one. Whether to take Diamox is a conversation for you and your own doctor before you fly, not for the mess tent at Urdukas.</p><h2><strong>Training for the Baltoro</strong></h2><p>People train for the altitude and are then defeated by the ground. The altitude is handled by the itinerary; the ground is handled by you.</p><p>Almost none of this route is a path. It is moraine: loose rock sitting on moving ice, boulder fields, debris slopes, meltwater channels and troughs, for eight days. Every step is slightly different from the last and nothing is level. It punishes ankles, knees and the small stabilising muscles that road running never touches, and it is the reason strong gym-fit trekkers sometimes struggle here while unspectacular hill walkers stroll through.</p><p><strong>Train on rough ground, with weight, for three to four months.</strong> Long days on hills carrying eight to ten kilograms, ideally on consecutive days at weekends so your legs learn what the fourth hour of the second day feels like. Walk on uneven trails rather than tarmac whenever you have the choice.</p><p>A practical benchmark: if you can walk eight hours with a ten-kilogram pack and a thousand metres of ascent, repeat it the following day, and still feel able to do a third, you are fit enough for this trek.</p><p>And break your boots in properly. Walking starts early, because afternoons are hotter and meltwater streams run higher. Lunch is carried from camp or cooked on the spot depending on the day. The pace is set to the slowest member of the group, which is the only safe way to run a trek at altitude. Camp is usually up by mid-afternoon, tea appears before the tents do, and the rest of the day is for washing, reading, dozing and sorting your bag. Dinner is three courses, and most people are asleep by eight.</p><p>A cook travels with the group for the entire trek with his own kitchen tent and assistants. The flight is flown on sight rather than instruments and is cancelled whenever the cloud is in, which is often; the road is reliable, spectacular, and long. We use whichever the weather allows and absorb the difference with the reserve day in the itinerary.</p><p>Because of that, two pieces of advice we give every guest. Do not book your international flight home for the same day the programme ends in Islamabad &mdash; leave a day. And buy a changeable ticket if your budget stretches to it. More journeys to Baltistan are disrupted by that one aircraft than by everything else combined, and none of it reflects how well matters are arranged on the ground.</p><h2><strong>If something goes wrong</strong></h2><p>A satellite phone and radios travel with every group of ours, not only with expeditions. Mobile coverage ends well before Askole; from there until you return, the satellite phone is the group&rsquo;s only link out for weather, logistics and emergencies, and the radios keep guide, crew and trekkers in contact across a long day spread out on the glacier.</p><p>Guides carry a comprehensive first aid kit and bottled oxygen. The treatment for serious altitude illness is to go down, immediately and with an escort, and on this route down is always available &mdash; which, structurally, makes this a safer high trek than one built around a pass.</p><p>Helicopter evacuation from the Baltoro is possible, it is coordinated from Skardu, and it is arranged through your insurer.
