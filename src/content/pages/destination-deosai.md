<h2><strong>The Land of Giants</strong></h2>
<p>Deosai is the second-highest alpine plateau in the world, averaging around 4,114m and spreading between the Skardu and Astore districts. The Balti name, Ghbiarsa, means summer's place; the local name Deosai is usually translated as the land of giants. Both fit. For eight months of the year it lies under snow and nobody crosses it; for a few weeks in summer it becomes an ocean of grass and wildflowers crossed by clear streams, with no trees anywhere and the horizon further away than anywhere else in Pakistan.</p>
<p>It became a national park in 1993, largely to protect the Himalayan brown bear, whose small population here has slowly recovered since.</p>

<h2><strong>When it opens</strong></h2>
<p>This is the single most important thing to know about Deosai: <strong>the road is seasonal</strong>. It usually opens in late May or June, once the snow has cleared, and closes again around late September or October with the first snowfall. The dates move every year. Outside that window the plateau simply cannot be reached by vehicle, and no operator can change that.</p>
<p>July is the peak — wildflowers across the plateau, the streams full, and the best chance of wildlife.</p>

<h2><strong>Getting there</strong></h2>
<p>Two approaches. From <strong>Skardu</strong> the road climbs south through Sadpara, past Sadpara Lake, and onto the plateau — around two to three hours to the park boundary. From <strong>Astore</strong> on the other side, the route comes up via Chilim and joins the same crossing, which makes it possible to traverse Deosai in one direction rather than returning the way you came.</p>
<p>A 4x4 with an experienced driver is essential. The tracks are rough, there are river fords, and there is no fuel, no shop and no mobile signal on the plateau.</p>

<h2><strong>What to see</strong></h2>
<p><strong>Sheosar Lake</strong> sits at about 4,142m near the Astore side, with Nanga Parbat visible beyond it on a clear day — one of the great views in the region.</p>
<p><strong>Wildlife.</strong> The Himalayan brown bear is the headline, and sightings do happen, most often early morning or evening, but nobody can promise them. Marmots are everywhere and almost comically unbothered by people; golden marmots, red fox, ibex on the surrounding slopes, and a long list of birds including golden eagle and lammergeier.</p>
<p><strong>The crossing itself</strong> is the experience — hours of open plateau, streams, and the Bara Pani bridge where most groups stop.</p>

<h2><strong>How to visit</strong></h2>
<p>A day trip from Skardu is possible but long. Two to three days is better, either camping on the plateau in summer or crossing through to Astore. Our <a href="/jeep-safaris/deosai-jeep-safari/">five-day Deosai jeep safari</a> takes the unhurried version from Skardu, and the <a href="/tours/deosai-khaplu-tour/">Deosai and Khaplu tour</a> combines the plateau with the Balti valleys.</p>

<h2><strong>Practical notes</strong></h2>
<p>At 4,000m the air is thin, and visitors who come straight up from Skardu sometimes feel it — headache, breathlessness, poor sleep. Spending a day or two in Skardu first helps. Nights are cold even in July, and weather changes quickly, so warm and waterproof layers are needed whatever the forecast said in town.</p>
<p>There is no infrastructure on the plateau: no shops, no fuel, no signal, and limited facilities. Everything is carried in and all waste must be carried out. Do not approach or feed wildlife, and stay in the vehicle if a bear is sighted — this is a protected and still-fragile population.</p>
