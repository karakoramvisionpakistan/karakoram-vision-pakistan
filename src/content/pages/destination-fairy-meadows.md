<h2><strong>A front-row seat at Nanga Parbat</strong></h2><p>Fairy Meadows is a shelf of alpine grassland at around 3,300m in the Raikot valley of Diamer district, facing directly onto the Raikot face of Nanga Parbat — at 8,126m the ninth highest mountain in the world. Locally the place is called Joot. The name travellers know came from German mountaineers who called it Märchenwiese, fairy-tale meadow, and it stuck after Hermann Buhl's first ascent of Nanga Parbat in 1953. The area was declared a national park in 1995.</p><p>There is nowhere else in Pakistan where an 8,000m face can be seen this closely for this little effort.</p><h2><strong>Getting there</strong></h2><p>The turn-off is at <strong>Raikot Bridge</strong> on the Karakoram Highway, roughly eight to ten hours from Islamabad or about two hours from Gilgit. From the bridge a jeep track of about fifteen kilometres climbs to Tato village. It is narrow, unsealed and exposed, it is driven only by local drivers who know it, and it takes an hour or more.</p><p>From Tato it is a <strong>walk of two to three hours</strong> uphill through pine forest to the meadows. Porters and ponies are available at Tato for bags. There is no road to Fairy Meadows itself, and that is part of why it stays as it is.</p><h2><strong>What to do once you are there</strong></h2><p>Most people spend the first evening simply watching the mountain change colour. Beyond the meadows the path continues to <strong>Beyal camp</strong>, about two hours further, and from there to the viewpoint above the <strong>Nanga Parbat base camp</strong> area — a long day walk from Fairy Meadows and back, or an easier two days with a night at Beyal.</p><p>The Raikot glacier runs below the meadow, and there are shorter walks to viewpoints along its edge for those who would rather not go all the way up.</p><h2><strong>When to go</strong></h2><p>The jeep road is generally open from <strong>May to October</strong>, depending on snow. June to September gives the most settled weather and the clearest views. Mornings are usually clearest; clouds often build on the face during the afternoon. By late October the huts begin to close for winter.</p><h2><strong>Where to stay</strong></h2><p>Accommodation is simple: wooden huts and tented camps on the meadow, with a stove for warmth and limited electricity, usually from a generator or solar for a few hours in the evening. Food is basic and cooked fresh. Nights are cold at 3,300m even in July, so a warm layer and a good sleeping bag or blankets matter.</p><h2><strong>Practical notes</strong></h2><p>There is no mobile signal at the meadows and patchy coverage at Tato. Bring cash; nothing up there takes cards. The jeep road has a reputation and deserves respect — it is driven safely every day by local drivers, but nervous passengers may prefer to walk the last section.</p><p>Carry out everything you bring in.
